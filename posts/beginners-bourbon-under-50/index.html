A Beginner's Guide to Bourbon Under $50

bourbonbeginnersshopping

You do not need a rare bottle or a tasting-club membership to understand bourbon. Some of the most instructive bottles on the shelf cost less than a nice dinner out. This guide covers what bourbon is, how to read a label, and which widely available bottles under $50 are useful starting points. Prices move by state, store, and season — including in Wisconsin — so treat every figure here as a typical range, not a promise.

What makes bourbon bourbon

Under U.S. federal rules, bourbon has to be made in the United States from a grain mixture that is at least 51 percent corn. It must be distilled to no more than 160 proof (80 percent alcohol by volume), enter a new charred oak container at no more than 125 proof, and be bottled at no less than 80 proof. Flavoring, coloring, and other additives are not allowed. Water is. New charred oak is the main source of vanilla, caramel, spice, and color. Corn supplies sweetness. The rest of the mash bill — usually rye or wheat, plus a little malted barley — changes the accent: rye often reads as spice or dryness, wheat as softer bread and caramel.

Two label phrases are worth memorizing.

Straight bourbon means the whiskey has been aged at least two years and meets the additive rules above. If it is less than four years old, the label must state the age.

Bottled-in-bond is an old consumer-protection standard from the Bottled-in-Bond Act of 1897. A bonded bourbon is straight, aged at least four years in a bonded warehouse, bottled at exactly 100 proof, and the product of one distillery in one distilling season (spring or fall). The distillery has to be named. For a beginner, bonded bottles are one of the most reliable quality floors on a budget.

Kentucky produces the large majority of bourbon, but bourbon can legally be made in any U.S. state. “Kentucky bourbon” on a label has additional state requirements.

How to read the rest of the label

Proof is twice the alcohol by volume. An 80-proof bourbon is 40 percent ABV; 100 proof is 50 percent. Higher proof is not automatically better, but it is more intense, and a few drops of water will often open it up.

Age statements tell you the age of the youngest whiskey in the bottle. No age statement is not inherently a warning — many solid everyday bottles do not carry one — but it also means you cannot use age as a comparison point.

Mash bill is not always disclosed. “High-rye” generally means a noticeable rye share; “wheated” means wheat is the flavoring grain instead of rye. “Single barrel” and “small batch” are looser terms than bottled-in-bond: the first means one barrel, so bottles can vary; the second usually means a limited blend, but the number is up to the producer.

A note on bottles that look cheap and are not

Several well-known names have a suggested price that can sit under $50, then sell for much more because supply is tight. Buffalo Trace, Blanton’s, and various Weller expressions are common examples. If a store has one at a true everyday price, fine. If the only way to get it is a raffle or a markup, skip it for now. Build a beginner shelf from bottles you can actually replace.

Everyday bottles worth knowing

The list below is not a ranking. These are widely distributed bourbons commonly found under $50 that illustrate different parts of the category. Confirm the price on the shelf in front of you.

Evan Williams Bottled-in-Bond (Heaven Hill). 100 proof, at least four years old, and usually among the least expensive bonded bourbons on the shelf. If you only buy one practice bottle, this is a strong candidate.

Old Grand-Dad Bonded (Beam Suntory). Also 100 proof and bonded, but built on a higher-rye mash bill than many Beam products. A useful contrast if you want more spice and dryness at a similar price. Prefer the bonded version over the lower-proof one as a study bottle.

Wild Turkey 101. Easy to find at 101 proof, and a clear lesson in what a little extra strength does in a glass and in a mixed drink.

Four Roses Yellow Label or Small Batch. Four Roses combines multiple mash bills and yeast strains. Yellow Label is the lower-proof introduction; Small Batch, often still under $50, is more concentrated.

Maker’s Mark. The familiar wax-dipped wheated bourbon, usually 90 proof. Compared with a high-rye bottle, it often comes across as softer and more confectionary, and it is easy to find.

Elijah Craig Small Batch (Heaven Hill). Typically 94 proof, often priced in the $30s, and a step up in oak and intensity from the cheapest bonded options without jumping into allocated territory.

Old Forester 100 or Knob Creek 9 Year. Old Forester 100 is another approachable 100-proof option. Knob Creek’s 9-year, 100-proof expression is often in the $30–$45 range; if it has drifted over $50 in your store, buy a bonded bottle instead.

You do not need all of these. A practical starter trio is one bonded bourbon (Evan Williams or Old Grand-Dad), one wheated bourbon (Maker’s Mark or, at a normal price, Larceny), and one slightly higher-intensity bottle (Wild Turkey 101 or Elijah Craig Small Batch). That gives you a map: proof, rye versus wheat, and a little more oak.

How to taste without making a ceremony of it

Use a small glass — a glencairn is nice; a juice glass is fine — and pour a modest amount. Smell it with your mouth slightly open, then take a small sip. Notice heat, sweetness, spice, oak, and bitterness, then take a second sip. If it feels closed or harsh, add a few drops of water, not a splash.

Do not judge a bottle only in an old fashioned. Sugar and bitters hide a lot. Try it neat first; a 100-proof bourbon often holds up better in mixed drinks than an 80-proof one. A short note such as “hot, sweet, a little cinnamon, better with water” is enough.

Buying in Wisconsin, or anywhere else

Shop a store with posted prices. Watch for damage, leaking wax, or a bottle sitting in a sunny window. Store bourbon upright, away from heat and light. An unopened bottle keeps for years. An opened one oxidizes slowly; it will usually be fine for many months, but it will not improve the way some wines can, and it should not be treated as an investment.

If you are not 21, this is not for you. If you are, drink slowly and do not drive.

A short shopping checklist

  1. Confirm the bottle is actually bourbon and note the proof.
  2. Prefer an age statement or a bottled-in-bond label when the price is otherwise similar.
  3. Skip markups on allocated names while you are still learning.
  4. Buy one bottle, taste it over a week, then buy a contrasting one — not a case.

Bourbon gets mystified because some of it is scarce and some of it is expensive. The part that matters at the beginning is more ordinary: corn, new charred oak, time, and proof. You can study all four for less than $50 a bottle.
