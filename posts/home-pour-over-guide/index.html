How to Make Pour-Over Coffee at Home

coffeebrewingpour-over

Pour-over is just hot water passing through ground coffee in a filter. The reason people fuss over it is control: you decide the dose, the grind, the water temperature, and the pace of the pour, which is harder to do in a cheap automatic drip machine. You do not need a cafe setup to get a clean, repeatable cup.

What you actually need

A dripper and filters. Common shapes are the cone (Hario V60, a Melitta-style cone) and the flat bottom (Kalita Wave, and several other models). A Chemex is a larger, thicker-filter cone. All of them can make good coffee. Buy the filters that match the dripper. If you are starting from zero, a plastic or ceramic V60 or a simple Melitta cone is enough.

A kettle. A gooseneck kettle makes a slow, steady pour easier, especially on a cone. It is not mandatory. A standard kettle and a careful hand will do while you decide whether you like this enough to buy more gear.

A scale. Measuring coffee and water by weight is the single biggest upgrade over scoops. A small digital scale that reads in grams, ideally with a timer, is enough.

A grinder. This is the part that most changes the cup. Blade grinders chop unevenly. A burr grinder, even an inexpensive hand grinder, produces a more consistent particle size, which means a more even extraction. If your budget has room for only one “nice” tool, make it the grinder.

Coffee and water. Use coffee roasted within the past few weeks, not a bag that has been open on a shelf for months. Store it in a sealed container away from heat and light, and grind it just before you brew. Water should taste clean. If your tap water is very hard, very soft, or strongly chlorinated, a simple filtered pitcher can help. You are extracting a large part of the cup from the water itself.

You also need a mug or a carafe to brew into, and a way to get water hot.

A starting recipe

A reliable place to begin is a ratio of about 1:16 — one gram of coffee to sixteen grams of water. That sits in the same neighborhood as the Specialty Coffee Association’s brewing guidelines, which describe a range rather than a single magic number. For one mug, 20 grams of coffee and 320 grams of water is a practical starting point. If you want a slightly stronger cup, try 1:15 (20 grams to 300 grams). If it tastes heavy or harsh, move toward 1:17.

Water temperature: just off boil is fine for most filter coffee, roughly 90–96°C (195–205°F). If you do not have a thermometer, bring the kettle to a boil and wait about 30 seconds. Very dark roasts can taste less bitter with slightly cooler water; very light roasts often do better at the hotter end.

Grind: medium-fine, like table salt, for a cone dripper. Flat-bottom drippers often want something a little coarser. You will adjust this more than anything else.

Total brew time for a 20-gram V60-style cup is commonly in the 2:30 to 3:30 minute range, including the bloom. Treat that as a checkpoint, not a law. The taste of the cup matters more than the stopwatch.

Step by step

  1. Heat your water. Rinse the paper filter in the dripper with hot water. This helps the filter sit against the walls, warms the dripper and carafe, and washes out loose paper flavor. Discard the rinse water.

  2. Grind 20 grams of coffee and add it to the filter. Level the bed with a gentle shake. Put the dripper and mug on the scale and tare it to zero.

  3. Bloom. Pour enough water to wet all the grounds, usually about twice the weight of the coffee (around 40 grams of water for a 20-gram dose). The coffee will swell and bubble as carbon dioxide escapes, especially if it is freshly roasted. Wait 30 to 45 seconds. If the bloom is lively, the coffee is probably fresh. If nothing happens, the coffee may be stale, but you can still brew it.

  4. Pour the rest of the water in two or three slow additions, keeping the water level reasonably steady and the grounds evenly wet. On a cone, many people pour in a spiral from the center outward, avoiding a hard stream against the paper wall. You do not need a theatrical pattern. You do need to avoid drowning one side of the bed and leaving the other dry. Finish at 320 grams.

  5. Let the water drain through. When the drip slows to occasional drops, you can remove the dripper. Give the coffee a swirl in the mug or carafe, then taste it after a minute or two, once it has cooled slightly. Very hot coffee hides both sweetness and faults.

How to fix a cup you do not like

Taste first, then change one variable at a time.

If the coffee tastes sour, thin, or salty in an unpleasant way, it is often under-extracted. Grind finer, pour a little more slowly, or use slightly hotter water.

If it tastes bitter, astringent, or hollow and dry, it is often over-extracted, or the grind is producing too much fine dust. Grind coarser, pour a little faster, or drop the water temperature slightly.

If the brew stalls and sits in the dripper for many minutes, the grind is probably too fine, the filter may have a bed of fines clogging it, or you may have compacted the coffee by pouring from too high or stirring too aggressively.

If the cup is weak but not sour, you may simply be using too little coffee for the amount of water. Tighten the ratio before you overhaul the grind.

Write down the coffee, dose, water, grind setting, and a few words about the result. The point of pour-over is not to memorize someone else’s choreography. It is to get a cup you can repeat on a Wednesday morning.

Gear that can wait

You do not need a temperature-controlled kettle or a custom brewing stand to make good filter coffee. Start with a dripper, a scale, a burr grinder, and fresh coffee. Learn the taste of a 1:16 cup on your water, then decide whether the next purchase is a better grinder — usually the right answer — or a different dripper.

A few habits that matter more than technique videos

Use a consistent dose. Rinse the filter. Grind fresh and keep the grinder clean; old oils go rancid. Drink the coffee the same morning if you can. If you make more than you will drink, a pre-warmed insulated carafe is kinder than a hot plate.

Pour-over has a reputation for fussy ritual. The ritual is optional. The useful core is small: weigh the coffee, weigh the water, grind evenly, and adjust based on what you taste.
